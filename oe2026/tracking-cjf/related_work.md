## Related Work and References

Our ontology draws on six bodies of work. Argumentation and deliberation models give us the vocabulary of moves and relations. Dialogue-act schemes and meeting corpora give us annotation practice. Theories of collective judgment and deliberation tell us what is worth capturing. Provenance and annotation standards let us tie each claim back to the transcript. Semantic-web patterns for state and time shape the StateAssignment history. Work on dialogue state tracking and facilitation agents sets out what our motivating application, a multiparty conversational agent (MPCA), needs to know. Across all six, the gap we address is the same: no existing model records, for each move in a live deliberation, how its status changed over time, which utterance changed it and which relationship triggered the change.

The page groups 35 references into six categories: [argumentation and deliberation models](#argumentation-models), [dialogue-act schemes and meeting corpora](#dialogue-acts), [collective judgment and deliberation theory](#collective-judgment), [provenance and text anchoring](#provenance), [state and change in knowledge graphs](#state-and-change), and [dialogue state tracking and facilitation agents](#facilitation-agents). Each entry gives a Chicago-style citation followed by a short note on how our work relates to it.

### 1. Argumentation, issue-based and deliberation models {#argumentation-models}

These models describe the *structure* of a discussion: which moves were made and how they relate. Our move types descend from them. Issue and Option follow IBIS and QOC. Our reified Support and Challenge relationships play the role of AIF's scheme nodes. Our ontology is designed to sit alongside the Deliberation Ontology (DEL) rather than replace it. None of these models records how a move's status evolves during the conversation, and none links moves to the transcript spans that justify them.

**Kunz, Werner, and Horst W. J. Rittel. 1970.** "Issues as Elements of Information Systems." Working Paper 131. Berkeley: Institute of Urban and Regional Development, University of California. [https://escholarship.org/uc/item/5cj786v8](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/5cj786v8).<br>
This paper introduced Issue-Based Information Systems (IBIS), which structure planning and design discussion as issues, positions that respond to them, and arguments for or against the positions. Our Issue, Option and Claim classes and our Support and Challenge relationships follow this model directly. IBIS treats an issue as settled only informally. Our ontology makes that status explicit and records each change with the utterance that caused it.

**Conklin, Jeff, and Michael L. Begeman. 1988.** "gIBIS: A Hypertext Tool for Exploratory Policy Discussion." *ACM Transactions on Information Systems* 6 (4): 303–331. [https://doi.org/10.1145/58566.59297](https://doi.org/10.1145/58566.59297).<br>
gIBIS put IBIS into a graphical hypertext tool: participants build a typed node-and-link map of the discussion as it happens. Our ontology keeps the typed-graph idea but builds the graph from annotated transcripts after the fact. Every node is anchored to a speaker's utterance rather than drawn by the participants.

**MacLean, Allan, Richard M. Young, Victoria M. E. Bellotti, and Thomas P. Moran. 1991.** "Questions, Options, and Criteria: Elements of Design Space Analysis." *Human–Computer Interaction* 6 (3–4): 201–250. [https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0603&4_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0603&4_2).<br>
QOC represents design rationale as questions, the options that answer them, and criteria for assessing those options. It is the direct precedent for our Option class and for distinguishing selected from rejected options. QOC records rationale after the decision. Our ontology tracks an option moving from UnderConsideration to Selected or Rejected during the conversation.

**Chesñevar, Carlos, Jarred McGinnis, Sanjay Modgil, Iyad Rahwan, Chris Reed, Guillermo Simari, Matthew South, Gerard Vreeswijk, and Steven Willmott. 2006.** "Towards an Argument Interchange Format." *The Knowledge Engineering Review* 21 (4): 293–316. [https://doi.org/10.1017/S0269888906001044](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0269888906001044).<br>
The Argument Interchange Format (AIF) is an abstract ontology for exchanging arguments between tools. Information nodes carry content, and scheme nodes represent inference, conflict and preference. Our reified relationships (Support, Challenge and others) play a similar role to scheme nodes, since each relationship is a node that can carry evidence. AIF models argument structure, but not the evolving state of moves in a conversation.

**Rahwan, Iyad, Fouad Zablith, and Chris Reed. 2007.** "Laying the Foundations for a World Wide Argument Web." *Artificial Intelligence* 171 (10–15): 897–921. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2007.04.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2007.04.015).<br>
This paper gave AIF a Semantic Web form in RDF Schema and OWL and built a system for publishing and querying arguments online. Our ontology follows the same publish-as-OWL approach. It adds transcript provenance and a state history that this argument web does not model.

**Lange, Christoph, Uldis Bojārs, Tudor Groza, John Breslin, and Siegfried Handschuh. 2008.** "Expressing Argumentative Discussions in Social Media Sites." In *Proceedings of the First Workshop on Social Data on the Web (SDoW2008)*, edited by John Breslin, Uldis Bojārs, Alexandre Passant, and Sergio Fernández. CEUR Workshop Proceedings 405. [https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-405/paper4.pdf](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-405/paper4.pdf).<br>
This paper introduced the SIOC Argumentation module, which adds IBIS-style issues, positions, ideas, arguments and decisions to SIOC's model of online posts. Its move types overlap with ours. It records that a decision exists but not the history of how an issue or idea reached it.

**Vagnoni, Simone, and Víctor Rodríguez-Doncel. 2025.** "A Deliberation Knowledge Graph: Bridging Institutional and Civic Democratic Discourse." In *Electronic Government and the Information Systems Perspective*, edited by Andrea Kő, Francesco Buccafurri, Gabriele Kotsis, A Min Tjoa, and Ismail Khalil, 122–136. Lecture Notes in Computer Science. Cham: Springer. [https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-02225-7_9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-02225-7_9).<br>
This paper presents the Deliberation Knowledge Graph and its Deliberation Ontology (DEL). DEL integrates parliamentary debates, e-participation platforms and deliberation datasets, and aligns with SIOC, SIOC Argumentation and AIF. DEL is the closest existing deliberation ontology. As described in the paper, however, it has no class for proposals or options, no status for issues or proposals, and no link from contributions to text spans. Our ontology fills exactly those gaps, so it complements DEL rather than competing with it.

**Vagnoni, Simone. n.d.** "Deliberation Knowledge Graph." Project website. Accessed November 27, 2026. [https://dkg.svagnoni.linkeddata.es/](https://dkg.svagnoni.linkeddata.es/).<br>
The project site publishes the knowledge graph, its ontology documentation, a SPARQL endpoint and conversion tools. It shows how a deliberation resource is released as linked data, which is a model for releasing our own annotated corpus. We align with DEL's terms where they overlap with ours.

**Barandiaran, Xabier E., Antonio Calleja-López, Arnau Monterde, and Carol Romero. 2024.** *Decidim, a Technopolitical Network for Participatory Democracy: Philosophy, Practice and Autonomy of a Collective Platform in the Age of Digital Intelligence.* SpringerBriefs in Political Science. Cham: Springer. [https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-50784-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-50784-7).<br>
This book describes Decidim, the open-source participatory-democracy platform first developed in Barcelona. Its proposals component gives each proposal an official answer status, such as evaluating, accepted or rejected ([Decidim documentation](https://docs.decidim.org/en/admin/components/proposals/answers.html)). That shows real deliberation systems need status tracking. Decidim keeps that status as application data with no evidence trail, whereas our ontology records it in the ontology together with the supporting utterance.

### 2. Dialogue-act schemes and meeting corpora {#dialogue-acts}

Dialogue-act schemes label utterances by their communicative function, and meeting corpora show how to annotate multiparty talk at scale. Our human annotation of transcripts builds on this practice. Our labels are coarser and specific to deliberation. Our annotations are also *links*, between moves and between a move and its state changes, rather than one tag per utterance. The decision-detection scheme of Fernández et al. is the closest precedent.

**Stolcke, Andreas, Klaus Ries, Noah Coccaro, Elizabeth Shriberg, Rebecca Bates, Daniel Jurafsky, Paul Taylor, Rachel Martin, Carol Van Ess-Dykema, and Marie Meteer. 2000.** "Dialogue Act Modeling for Automatic Tagging and Recognition of Conversational Speech." *Computational Linguistics* 26 (3): 339–373. [https://doi.org/10.1162/089120100561737](https://doi.org/10.1162/089120100561737).<br>
This study annotated the Switchboard telephone corpus with a set of 42 dialogue-act tags and trained statistical models to tag utterances automatically from words and prosody. It set the pattern of one function label per utterance, which most later work follows. Our ontology instead annotates deliberative function plus the relationships between moves, because a claim's role depends on what it supports or challenges.

**Bunt, Harry, Jan Alexandersson, Jae-Woong Choe, Alex Chengyu Fang, Koiti Hasida, Volha Petukhova, Andrei Popescu-Belis, and David Traum. 2012.** "ISO 24617-2: A Semantically-Based Standard for Dialogue Annotation." In *Proceedings of the Eighth International Conference on Language Resources and Evaluation (LREC 2012)*, 430–437. Istanbul: European Language Resources Association. [https://doi.org/10.63317/2vxg5i9ctqt8](https://doi.org/10.63317/2vxg5i9ctqt8).<br>
ISO 24617-2 is the international standard for dialogue-act annotation. It is multidimensional: one utterance can serve several functions. It also records dependence relations, for example an answer pointing to its question. Our InformationRequest class and Answer relationship correspond to its information-seeking functions and dependence links. We add argumentative relationships and a state history, which are outside the standard's scope.

**Carletta, Jean. 2007.** "Unleashing the Killer Corpus: Experiences in Creating the Multi-Everything AMI Meeting Corpus." *Language Resources and Evaluation* 41 (2): 181–190. [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10579-007-9040-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10579-007-9040-x).<br>
This paper reports on building the AMI Meeting Corpus, about 100 hours of recorded meetings with many layers of hand annotation, and on the practical lessons learned. It is the reference point for annotating multiparty meetings, and it shows the cost of rich, layered annotation. Our annotation targets a layer AMI does not cover: deliberative moves and their changing state, delivered as RDF.

**Fernández, Raquel, Matthew Frampton, Patrick Ehlen, Matthew Purver, and Stanley Peters. 2008.** "Modelling and Detecting Decisions in Multi-party Dialogue." In *Proceedings of the 9th SIGdial Workshop on Discourse and Dialogue*, edited by David Schlangen and Beth Ann Hockey, 156–163. Columbus, OH: Association for Computational Linguistics. [https://aclanthology.org/W08-0125/](https://aclanthology.org/W08-0125/).<br>
Working on AMI meetings, this paper defines decision-related dialogue acts: issues, resolution proposals, restatements of a resolution, and agreements. It then detects decision discussions automatically. These categories map closely onto our Issue, Option, Outcome and ExpressedPosition classes. They locate decisions. Our ontology also records how each issue and option got there.

### 3. Collective judgment and group deliberation theory {#collective-judgment}

This work defines what our ontology is trying to capture. Mallick et al. (2026) supply the concept of collective judgment formation that the project is named after. Xiromeriti (2025) characterizes deliberation as shared reasoning. Classic results on judgment aggregation, information pooling, deliberation dialogue and grounding identify what a record of group reasoning must preserve. Our ontology turns these ideas into a queryable graph built from real transcripts.

**Mallick, Rohit, Susannah B. F. Paletz, Aimée A. Kane, Kimberly Do, Adam Porter, Madeline Diep, and Ciara A. Fabian. 2026.** "Model of Collective Judgment Formation for Sequential Teams in Information-Dense Scenarios." *Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting*. Published online August 6, 2026. [https://doi.org/10.1177/10711813261475151](https://doi.org/10.1177/10711813261475151).<br>
This paper presents a descriptive model of collective judgment formation (CJF) in sequential teams, where members work in shifts and hand work over, in domains such as intelligence analysis and healthcare. In the model, judgments take shape from information supplied by humans and AI as pieces arrive and change. Our ontology gives that process a formal, inspectable representation at the level of a single conversation. Each judgment-relevant move and each change to it is recorded with its evidence.

**Xiromeriti, Vasiliki. 2025.** "What Is Collective Deliberation? Collective Deliberation as Shared Reasoning." *Episteme*, published online March 25, 2025, 1–15. [https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2025.14](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2025.14).<br>
Using Carnap's method of explication, Xiromeriti defines collective deliberation as shared reasoning, embedded in a group's joint activity and applied to questions that require argumentation. That definition matches our focus on issues, the options and claims raised in response, and the support and challenge relationships between them. Our ontology provides an empirical record against which such conceptual accounts can be tested.

**List, Christian, and Philip Pettit. 2002.** "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result." *Economics and Philosophy* 18 (1): 89–110. [https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266267102001098](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266267102001098).<br>
List and Pettit prove that no procedure for aggregating individual judgments on interconnected propositions can meet a small set of reasonable conditions. This result underlies the "discursive dilemma" between premise-based and conclusion-based group judgment. Aggregation theory abstracts away the conversation. Our ontology keeps it, recording which claims supported or challenged the option a group finally selected.

**Stasser, Garold, and William Titus. 1985.** "Pooling of Unshared Information in Group Decision Making: Biased Information Sampling During Discussion." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 48 (6): 1467–1478. [https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467).<br>
This classic "hidden profile" study showed that groups discuss information members already share more than information only one member holds, which can bias decisions. It motivates an agent that notices contributions the group has left behind. Our Open and Unassessed states make such neglected issues, requests and claims directly queryable.

**McBurney, Peter, David Hitchcock, and Simon Parsons. 2007.** "The Eightfold Way of Deliberation Dialogue." *International Journal of Intelligent Systems* 22 (1): 95–132. [https://doi.org/10.1002/int.20191](https://doi.org/10.1002/int.20191).<br>
This paper gives a formal model of deliberation dialogue between software agents, with eight stages that run from opening and informing through proposing, considering and revising to recommending, confirming and closing. Its stages correspond to our progression from Issue through Option to Outcome. Their model prescribes a protocol for agents. Ours describes, after the fact, what human groups actually did.

**Clark, Herbert H., and Susan E. Brennan. 1991.** "Grounding in Communication." In *Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition*, edited by Lauren B. Resnick, John M. Levine, and Stephanie D. Teasley, 127–149. Washington, DC: American Psychological Association. [https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006).<br>
Clark and Brennan describe grounding, the joint process by which conversation partners establish that they mutually understand what has been said. Grounding is the theoretical basis for treating a claim as accepted by the group rather than merely stated. Our ExpressedPosition moves (Agreement, Disagreement) and the Supported and Contested states are a coarse, annotatable proxy for that process.

### 4. Provenance, evidence and text anchoring {#provenance}

Our ontology reuses established provenance vocabularies so that every move, relationship and state assignment can be traced to a text span, an utterance and a speaker. PROV-O and SIO are reused directly. The Web Annotation Data Model and NIF solve the same text-anchoring problem in other communities, and they are natural alignment targets for our TextSpan class.

**Lebo, Timothy, Satya Sahoo, and Deborah McGuinness, eds. 2013.** "PROV-O: The PROV Ontology." W3C Recommendation, April 30, 2013. [https://www.w3.org/TR/2013/REC-prov-o-20130430/](https://www.w3.org/TR/2013/REC-prov-o-20130430/).<br>
PROV-O is the W3C OWL ontology for provenance: entities, the activities that generate them and the agents responsible. Our ontology reuses it to connect text spans, utterances and speakers, so that the derivation of every annotation can be queried. The W3C document also credits contributors Khalid Belhajjame, James Cheney, David Corsar, Daniel Garijo, Stian Soiland-Reyes, Stephan Zednik and Jun Zhao.

**Dumontier, Michel, Christopher J. O. Baker, Joachim Baran, Alison Callahan, Leonid Chepelev, José Cruz-Toledo, Nicholas R. Del Rio, Geraint Duck, Laura I. Furlong, Nichealla Keath, Dana Klassen, Jamie P. McCusker, Núria Queralt-Rosinach, Matthias Samwald, Natalia Villanueva-Rosales, Mark D. Wilkinson, and Robert Hoehndorf. 2014.** "The Semanticscience Integrated Ontology (SIO) for Biomedical Research and Knowledge Discovery." *Journal of Biomedical Semantics* 5 (1): 14. [https://doi.org/10.1186/2041-1480-5-14](https://doi.org/10.1186/2041-1480-5-14).<br>
SIO is a general upper-level ontology of objects, processes, attributes and their relations, developed for integrating biomedical data. Our ontology reuses SIO's general-purpose terms for transcript structure. That gives our transcript model well-defined, widely reused relations instead of new ad hoc ones.

**Sanderson, Robert, Paolo Ciccarese, and Benjamin Young, eds. 2017.** "Web Annotation Data Model." W3C Recommendation, February 23, 2017. [https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/REC-annotation-model-20170223/](https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/REC-annotation-model-20170223/).<br>
The Web Annotation Data Model links an annotation's body to a target resource, using selectors such as character positions or quoted text to point into that resource. Our TextSpan class does the same job for transcripts but is defined inside our ontology. Mapping TextSpan to Web Annotation selectors would let standard annotation tools display our evidence.

**Hellmann, Sebastian, Jens Lehmann, Sören Auer, and Martin Brümmer. 2013.** "Integrating NLP Using Linked Data." In *The Semantic Web – ISWC 2013*, edited by Harith Alani, Lalana Kagal, Achille Fokoue, Paul Groth, Chris Biemann, Josiane Xavier Parreira, Lora Aroyo, Natasha Noy, Chris Welty, and Krzysztof Janowicz, 98–113. Lecture Notes in Computer Science. Berlin: Springer. [https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-41338-4_7](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-41338-4_7).<br>
This paper defines the NLP Interchange Format (NIF), an RDF vocabulary that identifies text strings by character offsets so that the outputs of different NLP tools can be combined. NIF covers token- and phrase-level annotation, not discourse or deliberation structure. Our ontology could express its TextSpans as NIF strings and add the deliberation layer on top.

### 5. Representing state and change in knowledge graphs {#state-and-change}

The Semantic Web has general patterns for facts that change over time and for statements about statements, but none designed for dialogue. Our ontology combines two of them. Relationships and state assignments are reified as n-ary relation classes. Each move's assignments form a chain linked to the previous assignment, indexed by utterance rather than clock time. A SHACL rule derives the current assignment from that chain, so one graph answers both "what is the state now?" and "how did it get here?".

**Noy, Natasha, and Alan Rector, eds. 2006.** "Defining N-ary Relations on the Semantic Web." W3C Working Group Note, April 12, 2006. [https://www.w3.org/TR/2006/NOTE-swbp-n-aryRelations-20060412/](https://www.w3.org/TR/2006/NOTE-swbp-n-aryRelations-20060412/).<br>
This W3C note sets out the standard patterns for expressing relations among more than two things in RDF and OWL, mainly by turning the relation into a class of its own. Our Support, Challenge, Address, Resolve, Answer, Refinement and Position relationships, and our StateAssignment records, follow this pattern. That lets each one carry evidence, a reason and a triggering relationship.

**Welty, Christopher, and Richard Fikes. 2006.** "A Reusable Ontology for Fluents in OWL." In *Formal Ontology in Information Systems: Proceedings of the Fourth International Conference (FOIS 2006)*. Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence and Applications. Amsterdam: IOS Press. Earlier version: IBM Research Report RC23755, [https://dominoweb.draco.res.ibm.com/reports/rc23755.pdf](https://dominoweb.draco.res.ibm.com/reports/rc23755.pdf).<br>
This paper introduced the four-dimensional "fluents" pattern for OWL, in which time-varying relations hold between time-slices of entities. It is the best-known alternative to reification for change over time. We chose explicit StateAssignment records instead, because each change in our ontology needs its own evidence and cause, and in a conversation time is naturally measured in utterances.

**Cox, Simon, and Chris Little, eds. 2017.** "Time Ontology in OWL." W3C Recommendation, October 19, 2017. [https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/REC-owl-time-20171019/](https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/REC-owl-time-20171019/).<br>
OWL-Time is the W3C ontology of instants, intervals, durations and their ordering. Our ontology orders state changes by the sequence of utterances rather than by timestamps. OWL-Time is the natural alignment if transcripts with clock times are added later.

**Nguyen, Vinh, Olivier Bodenreider, and Amit Sheth. 2014.** "Don't Like RDF Reification? Making Statements About Statements Using Singleton Property." In *Proceedings of the 23rd International Conference on World Wide Web*, 759–770. New York: ACM. [https://doi.org/10.1145/2566486.2567973](https://doi.org/10.1145/2566486.2567973).<br>
This paper proposes singleton properties, a unique property instance for each statement, as a lighter alternative to RDF reification for attaching metadata to triples. We considered statement-level metadata of this kind and chose class-based reification. Our relationships are first-class participants that trigger state changes and are themselves supported or challenged, not just annotated triples.

**Styles, Rob, and Nadeem Shabir. 2008.** "Lifecycle Schema." Talis Information Ltd. [https://vocab.org/lifecycle/schema](https://vocab.org/lifecycle/schema).<br>
The Lifecycle Schema is a small RDF vocabulary for describing the states and transitions that any resource may pass through. It is the closest existing vocabulary for resource status, but it describes the lifecycle and the current state, not a history of changes with their evidence. Our StateAssignment chain records every past state, its effective utterance and its cause.

**Knublauch, Holger, Dean Allemang, and Simon Steyskal, eds. 2017.** "SHACL Advanced Features." W3C Working Group Note, June 8, 2017. [https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/NOTE-shacl-af-20170608/](https://www.w3.org/TR/2017/NOTE-shacl-af-20170608/).<br>
SHACL Advanced Features extends the SHACL shapes language with rules, custom targets and functions. Our ontology uses a SHACL rule to pick out each move's current state assignment from its history. This keeps the "current state" query simple while the full history stays in the graph.

### 6. Dialogue state tracking, common ground and facilitation agents {#facilitation-agents}

Our motivating application is a multiparty conversational agent that follows a group's reasoning and decides when to step in. Dialogue state tracking follows one user's task goals. Common-ground tracking follows what all participants accept. Facilitation agents decide when to act mostly from discussion phase, elapsed time and participation balance. Our ontology supplies the missing input: which issues remain open, which claims are contested and which options are still under consideration, each traceable to the transcript.

**Traum, David R., and Staffan Larsson. 2003.** "The Information State Approach to Dialogue Management." In *Current and New Directions in Discourse and Dialogue*, edited by Jan van Kuppevelt and Ronnie W. Smith, 325–353. Text, Speech and Language Technology. Dordrecht: Springer. [https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-010-0019-2_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-010-0019-2_15).<br>
In the information-state approach, a dialogue system's knowledge is kept in an explicit information state that dialogue moves update through rules. Our StateAssignments and the SHACL rule play an analogous role. The state belongs to the group's deliberation, not to one system, and it is kept as a knowledge graph with full history.

**Williams, Jason D., Antoine Raux, and Matthew Henderson. 2016.** "The Dialog State Tracking Challenge Series: A Review." *Dialogue & Discourse* 7 (3): 4–33. [https://doi.org/10.5087/dad.2016.301](https://doi.org/10.5087/dad.2016.301).<br>
This review covers the Dialog State Tracking Challenges, in which systems estimate a user's goal, typically as values for task slots, from noisy task-oriented dialogue. Dialogue state tracking is the established form of state tracking in conversation. Our ontology moves the idea from one user's slots to a group's issues, options and claims.

**Khebour, Ibrahim Khalil, Kenneth Lai, Mariah Bradford, Yifan Zhu, Richard A. Brutti, Christopher Tam, Jingxuan Tu, Benjamin A. Ibarra, Nathaniel Blanchard, Nikhil Krishnaswamy, and James Pustejovsky. 2024.** "Common Ground Tracking in Multimodal Dialogue." In *Proceedings of the 2024 Joint International Conference on Computational Linguistics, Language Resources and Evaluation (LREC-COLING 2024)*, edited by Nicoletta Calzolari, Min-Yen Kan, Veronique Hoste, Alessandro Lenci, Sakriani Sakti, and Nianwen Xue, 3587–3602. Torino: ELRA and ICCL. [https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.318/](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.318/).<br>
This paper defines common ground tracking: identifying which task-relevant propositions all participants in a group accept. It does so with moves such as statement, accept, doubt, question and answer, which update separate banks of open questions, evidence and facts. It is the closest formal precedent for our Unassessed-to-Supported or Contested and Open-to-Answered transitions. Their setting is a closed, in-person task. Ours is open-ended deliberation in which the outcome is a group judgment.

**Kim, Soomin, Jinsu Eun, Changhoon Oh, Bongwon Suh, and Joonhwan Lee. 2020.** "Bot in the Bunch: Facilitating Group Chat Discussion by Improving Efficiency and Participation with a Chatbot." In *Proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, 1–13. New York: ACM. [https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376785](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376785).<br>
This paper presents GroupfeedBot, a chatbot that manages discussion time, encourages even participation and organizes members' opinions in group chats. It was evaluated against groups with no bot. It shows that an in-chat agent can improve how groups discuss. Its interventions do not draw on a model of which issues or claims are still unresolved, which is the state our ontology provides.

**Kim, Soomin, Jinsu Eun, Joseph Seering, and Joonhwan Lee. 2021.** "Moderator Chatbot for Deliberative Discussion: Effects of Discussion Structure and Discussant Facilitation." *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* 5 (CSCW1): 1–26. [https://doi.org/10.1145/3449161](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449161).<br>
DebateBot structures online deliberation into stages and prompts quieter participants. In a controlled experiment, structure improved perceived deliberative quality and facilitation produced more authentic consensus. The study supports the MPCA premise that an agent can partly substitute for a human moderator. Our ontology would give such an agent an explicit picture of where the deliberation stands to base its interventions on.

**Tessler, Michael Henry, Michiel A. Bakker, Daniel Jarrett, Hannah Sheahan, Martin J. Chadwick, Raphael Koster, Georgina Evans, Lucy Campbell-Gillingham, Tantum Collins, David C. Parkes, Matthew Botvinick, and Christopher Summerfield. 2024.** "AI Can Help Humans Find Common Ground in Democratic Deliberation." *Science* 386 (6719): eadq2852. [https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq2852](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq2852).<br>
In this study an AI mediator, the "Habermas Machine", drafted group statements from participants' written opinions and revised them using their critiques. Participants preferred its statements to those of human mediators, and within-group disagreement fell. The system works with free-text opinions and statements rather than an explicit model of issues, claims and their status. Our ontology is that explicit record, which also preserves the disagreements a consensus statement can hide.
